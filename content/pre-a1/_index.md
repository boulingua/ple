## Why a stage before A1

Most CEFR courses start at A1 and teach pronunciation incidentally, a remark at
a time, inside units that are really about greetings or family. For learners
whose first language is German that is a poor bargain. Portuguese —
European Portuguese in particular — front-loads several difficulties that do
not resolve themselves through exposure alone:

- **Nasal vowels.** *ão, ãe, õe, ã, õ* and the nasal diphthongs have no German
  counterpart, and the contrast is functional: *pão* / *pau*, *mãe* / *mais*.
- **Unstressed vowel reduction.** European Portuguese reduces unstressed
  vowels heavily; *telefone* is not read as it is spelt. This is the single
  biggest obstacle to hearing word boundaries, and it is the reason listening
  feels disproportionately hard at A1.
- **The digraphs.** *lh* and *nh* are single palatal consonants, not
  *l + h* and *n + h*; the German reflex is to break them apart.
- **The cedilla and the sibilants.** *ç*, *s*, *ss*, *z*, *x*, *ch* and the
  positional value of *s* at the end of a syllable.
- **Stress and the accent marks.** *á â ã à é ê í ó ô õ ú* encode stress and
  vowel quality together. Learned as a system, they are a reading aid; learned
  one word at a time, they are noise.

Four short pages, each with audio for every sound discussed, dispose of these
before A1 begins. They are editorial reading, not units: no five-move sequence,
no exam.

## How to use this stage

**In class**, the four pages are roughly two lessons. Do not attempt to make
learners *produce* the nasal vowels accurately at this point; the goal is that
they can hear the contrast and can map a written accent to a stress position.
Production catches up across A1.

**Alone**, read each page with the audio, then reread the minimal pairs a day
later. The single highest-value habit is to listen to whole phrases rather than
isolated words: European Portuguese reduction only makes sense in connected
speech, and an isolated word is the least representative sample of it.

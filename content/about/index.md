## What this is

*Português Língua Estrangeira* (PLE) is a free, openly-licensed Portuguese
course written and maintained by one person, S. Le Boulanger, as part of the
**boulingua** family of language courses. It is built for the German
*Gesamtschule* system and for independent adult learners, and it is published
as an open educational resource: no account, no paywall, and a licence that
permits reuse and adaptation with attribution.

Portuguese is the sixth most widely spoken language in the world and an official
language in nine countries on four continents. It is also, in the German school
and adult-education landscape, badly under-served by free, CEFR-aligned material
of the kind that exists in abundance for English, French and Spanish. This
course exists to close a little of that gap for the levels where the gap does
most damage — the beginning.

## Who it is for

Two audiences, deliberately:

- **Teachers**, who need something they can take into a room: a unit with a
  timing, a deck, a worksheet, an answer key and a test that can be set without
  adaptation. Everything on this site is designed to be printed, projected,
  edited and reused.
- **Independent learners**, who need a coherent path rather than a pile of
  exercises: a stated level, a stated goal per unit, and a self-check at the end
  of it.

A learner with Romance-language experience — French, Spanish, Italian — will
find the transfer substantial, and the course is written with that transfer in
mind: it points out where Portuguese behaves as a French or Spanish speaker
expects, and, more often and more usefully, where it does not.

## Why European Portuguese

This is the decision that shapes every other decision in the course, so it is
stated plainly rather than left implicit.

**The course teaches European Portuguese (pt-PT), in the orthography of the 1990
*Acordo Ortográfico*.** The pronunciation modelled in the audio is Lisbon-based
standard European Portuguese; the pronoun system taught is the Portuguese one,
with *tu* as the ordinary informal second person; the vocabulary is the
vocabulary of Portugal.

The reason is destination realism. The learners this course is written for are
in Europe. The sister courses in this family serve the same school context, and
a learner who reaches B1 here is most likely to use the language in Portugal, in
a Portuguese workplace, or with a Portuguese-speaking community in Europe.

This is not a claim about which variety matters more. Roughly four in five
speakers of Portuguese are Brazilian, and Brazilian Portuguese is by any
demographic measure the dominant norm. It is a claim about what makes a course
teachable: a beginner needs **one** consistent model, not an average of two.
Teaching an averaged Portuguese that nobody speaks is the reliable way to
produce learners who are understood nowhere in particular.

So the course teaches one norm and is explicit about the other. **Every unit
carries a compact *No Brasil* box** recording the concrete divergences at that
point in the syllabus — *você* against *tu*, *a gente* against *nós*, the gerund
against *estar a* + infinitive, proclitic against enclitic pronoun placement,
and the everyday lexical splits (*comboio* / *trem*, *autocarro* / *ônibus*,
*pequeno-almoço* / *café da manhã*, *casa de banho* / *banheiro*). Those boxes
are kept structurally separate from the main body precisely so that a Brazilian
track could later be branched from the same source rather than rewritten from
scratch.

On orthography: the 1990 agreement reduced but did not eliminate divergence.
Forms such as *facto* / *fato* and *receção* / *recepção* still differ by norm,
and pre-reform spellings (*acção*, *óptimo*) are no longer used here. The style
guide fixes a word list, and a lint check refuses pre-reform forms.

## Level scope, and what is not claimed

The course targets **Pre-A1 through B1**. That is what a single author can write
and maintain to a standard worth publishing, and it is where free material is
scarcest.

- A **Pre-A1 stage**, *Sons & Sinais*, deals with the sound system and the
  spelling that encodes it before any grammar. Portuguese uses the Latin
  alphabet, so there is no script barrier — which is exactly why the sound
  system is so often neglected until it has already done damage to listening.
- **A1, A2 and B1** are each a run of roughly twelve units with a sibling model
  exam per unit.
- **B2 and above are not claimed.** They are recorded as a stretch phase. A
  complete, well-made A1–B1 is more useful than a thin A1–C1, and saying so is
  more honest than a coverage table with holes in it.

Against the boulingua curriculum framework the course declares conformance level
**`core`** — every in-scope CEFR scale that carries an official descriptor at
A1, A2 or B1 is implemented. Where a scale has no descriptor at a level — much
of mediation strategy, most of the plurilingual scales, all of them at A1 — the
course records `no-official-descriptor` rather than inventing coverage. Every
can-do claim in a unit resolves to a framework identifier of the form
`A1.INT.information-exchange.01`, and the build fails on any identifier that
does not resolve. The framework holds the grounded CEFR descriptor; the course
holds the Portuguese realisation. Neither reproduces Council of Europe text.

The course is **not** an examination preparation service and has no relationship
with any examining body. The model exams follow the published *shape* of
common A1–B1 formats — task count, task types, timings, marking criteria — with
stimulus texts written entirely for this course. No published examination
material is reproduced.

## How a unit is built

Every unit runs the same five moves — **Activate → Input → Practise → Apply →
Reflect** — and every unit is paired with an exam bundle that shares its number.
The five moves are not decoration: they let a teacher plan a double lesson
without reading the unit first, and they let a learner know where they are.

Unit prose, slide deck, worksheet and audio come from the same source. Decks and
worksheets are generated locally from branded LaTeX templates and committed to
the repository, so the deployment pipeline only verifies them; audio is
generated with a native European Portuguese voice through the shared `audiogen`
workflow and committed the same way.
